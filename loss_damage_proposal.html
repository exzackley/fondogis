1. Current Climate and Hazard Context

1.1 Structural Exposure to Tropical Cyclones

Mexico is structurally exposed to tropical cyclones due to its geographic position between the Eastern Pacific Ocean and the Atlantic basin, including the Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean Sea. Historical cyclone track data from the International Best Track Archive for Climate Stewardship (IBTrACS) show that storms frequently approach or make landfall along both Mexican coastlines, placing Mexico among the countries most frequently impacted by tropical cyclones globally.

Cyclone density maps developed by NOAA demonstrate a high concentration of storm activity in the Caribbean basin and along the Pacific coast of southern Mexico. The frequency of these hazards increases the cumulative risk faced by coastal ecosystems, fisheries, tourism infrastructure, and vulnerable communities. An Extended Study Area defined by Castillo et al. extracted all tropical cyclones (TCs) between 1970 and 2023 from IBTrACS. Out of approximately 1,600 TCs recorded in the region, only 266 made landfall, yet 305 TCs were associated with some disaster, regardless of whether they made landfall (Castillo et al., Tropical Cyclones in Mexico: A Comparative Analysis).

Storm surge represents one of the most destructive impacts associated with tropical cyclones. Global coastal flood hazard modelling based on the Global Tide and Surge Model (GTSM, Deltares) shows that extensive portions of Mexico’s coastline are exposed to surge-induced flooding. Low-lying coastal regions along the Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean are particularly vulnerable, including critical ecosystems such as mangroves, coastal lagoons, and wetlands that provide natural coastal protection but are increasingly under pressure from climate change and coastal development.

1.2 Observed Sea Level Rise

Satellite altimetry data confirm that sea levels are already rising across Mexico’s coastlines. The 39 coastal protected areas analyzed in this proposal show an average observed rate of — mm/year, with individual areas such as Los Petenes and Ría Lagartos exceeding 6 mm/year (IPCC AR6). Critically, 37 of 39 coastal ANPs exhibit sea level rise rates exceeding 0.5 mm/year. This threshold is significant because, for practical coastal planning purposes, annual water levels become materially dangerous when they exceed approximately 0.5 m above the local high-tide baseline—a threshold that sustained rates of this magnitude will reach within decades, compounding the impacts of storm surges, king tides, and coastal erosion on already vulnerable ecosystems and communities. 1.3 Current Temperature Baseline

Mean annual temperatures across these coastal protected areas currently range from approximately 22°C to 28°C (1981–2010 baseline period, NASA NEX-GDDP-CMIP6). These warm tropical and subtropical conditions already place significant thermal stress on marine and coastal ecosystems, particularly coral reefs and mangroves. 2. Documented Losses and Damages

This section presents evidence of climate-related losses and damages already experienced by communities within and adjacent to Mexico’s coastal protected areas. Data are drawn from three complementary sources: federal disaster and emergency declarations issued by Mexico’s National Civil Protection System (datos.gob.mx, 2018–2024), the CENAPRED historical disaster database (2000–2023), and the Emergency Events Database (EM-DAT).

Between 2018 and 2024, Mexico’s federal government issued a total of — disaster and emergency declarations affecting municipalities within — coastal protected areas. The CENAPRED historical record (2000–2023) further documents — deaths from hydrometeorological events in these same municipalities.

These are not future projections. The figures presented in this section represent losses and damages already incurred by communities living within and around Mexico’s coastal protected areas. The recurrent and cumulative nature of these impacts underscores the urgent need for dedicated financial mechanisms for loss and damage response.

2.1 Disaster Patterns and Trends

Historical disaster databases confirm that tropical cyclones have generated significant economic losses and social impacts in Mexico over recent decades. From the 34 disasters registered in EM-DAT in Mexico between 2020 and 2025, 12 occurred in the project’s target regions, most associated with tropical cyclones and floods, together affecting more than 112,027 people. National records from CENAPRED show that the actual frequency of hydrometeorological impacts is significantly higher, as many localized floods, landslides, and storm-related damages do not meet global database thresholds.

CENAPRED socioeconomic data (1980–2023) show that Guerrero recorded more than 200 TC-related fatalities, followed by Oaxaca and Veracruz—all states containing project target ANPs. —% of all declarations affecting coastal ANPs are linked to hydrometeorological phenomena—hurricanes, severe rainfall, and flooding—events directly intensified by climate change. 3. Climate Projections: Compounding Future Risk

The documented losses presented in Section 2 will intensify under climate change. CMIP6 climate models (NASA NEX-GDDP) project significant temperature increases across all 39 coastal protected areas by mid-century under scenario SSP2-4.5 (2041–2070), with northern areas such as Alto Golfo de California experiencing increases exceeding +2°C. Simultaneously, sea level rise is projected to accelerate, permanently altering coastal ecosystems that currently serve as natural buffers against storm surges and flooding.

The combination of rising temperatures, accelerating sea level rise, and increasing tropical cyclone intensity creates a compounding risk scenario that demands proactive financial mechanisms capable of addressing climate-related loss and damage before these projections materialize fully.

Figure 9 compares the current baseline mean annual temperature (1981–2010) with mid-century projections (2041–2070, SSP2-4.5) for all 39 coastal ANPs.
